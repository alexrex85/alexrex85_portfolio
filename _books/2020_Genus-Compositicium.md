In 2020, a research fellowship gave me the opportunity to revisit and substantially develop my [doctoral dissertation](https://hdl.handle.net/11390/1132330), originally written between 2011 and 2013.
The resulting work was published as a monograph in the prestigious series [Innsbrucker Beiträge zur Sprachwissenschaft (vol. 165)](https://hdl.handle.net/11390/1210943).

It represents a significant development of the theoretical framework adopted in my doctoral research, as it applies the principles of Construction Grammar to the study of Latin nominal compounds.
This approach makes possible to explore the relationship between the 'form' of a word and its meaning, and particularly in compounds, whose structure often reveals a close correspondence between the formal shape of the linguistic sign and its semantic value, in terms that recall Ferdinand de Saussure’s well-known distinction between *signifiant* and *signifié*.

The relationship between linguistic form and meaning had already attracted the attention of ancient scholars such as Plato and Varro.
While Plato considered the relationship between words and the concepts they designate to be essentially arbitrary, compound nouns suggest a more complex picture, since their internal structure often provides clues to their meaning.

Varro, in particular, recognized that the way in which simple words are combined to form the *genus compositicium* is not arbitrary, but follows specific principles.
These principles make possible both the creation of new words and the interpretation of existing compounds.
In discussing what he calls *declinatio voluntaria*, Varro also observed that speakers could potentially create words that, although not actually attested, would nevertheless be perfectly well formed according to the rules of the language.

The study of a selection of Latin authors from the first century AD to Late Antiquity, representing a wide range of literary genres — including epic poetry, satire, novel, historiography, and monograph — provided further evidence for the relationship between the type of compound, its formal structure, and its meaning.
Statistical analysis also showed that some patterns remained highly productive, giving rise to numerous neologisms in later authors, while others gradually fell out of use.

The research therefore suggests that grammatical constructions should not be regarded merely as abstract structures existing in the minds of speakers or authors.
They are also reflected concretely in linguistic expression, where they contribute in shaping the language of the major literary works transmitted to us from Antiquity.
